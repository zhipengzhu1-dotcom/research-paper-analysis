                                        Training Large Language Models for Small-Molecule Design with
                                                            Synthetic Task Scaling
                                                  Frank Hu,* Shriram Chennakesavalu,* Zichen Wang, Patricia Suriana, Bodhi Vani,
                                                                Kirill Shmilovich, Kangway Chuang, Colin Grambow
                                                                            Prescient Design, Genentech, South San Francisco, CA, USA

                                                                                              September 7, 2026




arXiv:2609.04735v1 [cs.LG] 4 Sep 2026
                                                                                                     Abstract
                                              Designing viable drug candidates requires searching a combinatorially large and rugged chemical space for
                                              molecules that satisfy multiple, often competing, objectives. Large language models (LLMs) provide a useful
                                              generative prior for this problem because of their representational capacity, reasoning ability, and flexibility when
                                              incorporating information from the external environment. While reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards
                                              (RLVR) can be used to improve the capabilities of LLMs, many chemically relevant scoring functions require hours
                                              or even days per evaluation, making them prohibitively expensive to use directly during online training. Here, we
                                              investigate whether LLMs can learn molecular design strategies from cheaper synthetic tasks that generalize to
                                              expensive molecular lead optimization settings. We find that curriculum-based training recipes that gradually
                                              incorporate more challenging synthetic design tasks enable strong performance that surpasses that of much larger
                                              frontier models on structure-based lead optimization. Our results suggest that scaling post-training using synthetic
                                              tasks is an effective strategy for adapting LLMs to high-cost experimental scenarios that are too expensive to directly
                                              train on.



                                        1 Introduction
                                        Designing molecules for drug discovery campaigns is a highly involved and complex process that involves searching
                                        over a vast and rugged chemical space for molecules that satisfy a set of property objectives and constraints. For
                                        many therapeutic modalities, these constraints often center around designing molecules with high affinity for a bio-
                                        logical target and satisfying certain chemical and physiological profiles, including those related to pharmacokinetics,
                                        developability, and toxicity (Lipinski, 2004; Gleeson, 2008; Bickerton et al., 2012).
                                             Many conventional search workflows follow a generation, scoring, and filtering order, where generated molecules
                                        are scored and filtered, resulting in a set of molecules with desired properties (Walters et al., 1998; Shoichet, 2004;
                                        Kitchen et al., 2004; Sadybekov and Katritch, 2023). These workflows use a range of scoring in silico oracles with varying
                                        cost and fidelity, ranging from cheap but low-fidelity docking oracles (Friesner et al., 2004; Trott and Olson, 2010; McGann,
                                        2011; McGann, 2012) to high-fidelity but expensive free energy perturbation (FEP) calculations (L. Wang et al., 2015;
                                        Ross et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2024). Given this cost of scoring and the often limited chemical space accessible to classic
                                        molecular generators, many of these workflows often search small pockets of chemical space. Developing molecular
                                        generators that can cheaply and robustly sample desired regions of chemical space is a longstanding challenge with
                                        immense practical value.
                                             Recently, large language models trained at scale have displayed remarkable representational capabilities, both
                                        in terms of their stored information (Morris et al., 2025) and their ability to reason about this information (Cobbe
                                        et al., 2021; Wei, X. Wang, et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023; OpenAI, 2024; Zeng et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025). Additionally, recent
                                        developments in “post-training” methods have made it possible to control the outputs of these models, ensuring
                                        that the model largely generates desired responses (Schulman et al., 2017; Rafailov et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Lambert
                                        et al., 2025). The combination of broad generative capacity and controllable generation via post-training makes
