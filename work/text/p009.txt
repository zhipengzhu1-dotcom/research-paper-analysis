    From Figure 3, we see that training on the simple Tier-1 tasks alone enables near-frontier-model-level per-
formance on a much harder task, suggesting that the model is learning general molecular design principles that
generalize to a much harder structure-based design task. Additionally, we see that the performance of all the
curriculum-trained models exceeds the performance of the T1(500) baseline and that many of the curriculum-
trained models exceed the performance of frontier models. Finally, we note that training on the Tier-2 tasks only
starting from the Qwen 3.6 (base) model leads to mode collapse. This model generates degenerate single-turn
policies that do not perform genuine optimization, further reinforcing the need for the curriculum training approach
(see Figure A1 and Appendix C.1).
    Next, we explore the relationship between performance and training cost, where we define cost as the total wall
time required for training given that all models were trained for the same number of steps using the same hardware
configuration (see Appendix B). Unsurprisingly, the T1(500) model was the cheapest to train in terms of total wall
time, with all of the curriculum-trained models being more expensive given that a full rollout for a single Tier-2 task
can take up to twice as long as a full rollout for a Tier-1 task. Notably, we do not see a clear “scaling law” between
training time and performance; training with more expensive recipes does not necessarily translate to improved
performance on the SQM lead optimization task.
    We do see that models trained fully on the Tier-2 environment in the second stage of training after initializing
from a T1(500) checkpoint are comparable to, or better performers than, their mixed counterparts. There is generally
no clear benefit to mixing in tasks from Tier-1 during the second stage of training, suggesting that there is minimal
risk of catastrophic forgetting during curriculum training. The performance of T1(300)–T2(200) and T1(400)–T2(100)
models is better than that of the T1(300)–Mix(200) and T1(400)–Mix(100) models, respectively, and we observe
comparable performance between the T1(350)–Mix(150)/T1(350)–T2(150) and T1(450)–Mix(50)/T1(450)–T2(50)
pairs. The T1(250)–Mix(250) model is an exception, where training on the mixed environments leads to significantly
better performance than T1(250)–T2(250).
