learning-based approaches that leverage verifiable rewards (RLVR) to train language models that can generate
molecules against complex design objectives and constraints (G. Ye et al., 2023; Fang et al., 2024; Narayanan et al.,
2025; Chennakesavalu, Shmilovich, et al., 2026). While useful, many of these efforts train models with simple and
cheaply evaluable chemical oracles that do not reflect the full complexity and cost of chemical oracles used in
real-world drug discovery efforts (e.g., FEP). As such, these models cannot effectively design molecules with desired
“high-fidelity” properties, limiting their utility in real-world drug discovery settings.
    Post-training LLMs using an RLVR framework with more expensive oracles is challenging due to the online nature
of training; expensive oracles can result in impractically long training times and/or severely stale rollouts when
carrying out RL training asynchronously (Prime Intellect, 2025; Noukhovitch et al., 2025; GLM-5-Team et al., 2026). In this
work, we ask whether we can post-train LLMs to be capable small-molecule designers for expensive, high-fidelity
objectives by scaling training on cheaper synthetic molecular design tasks. In doing so, we investigate whether
models can learn foundational molecular design principles on cheap tasks that can then enable more efficient
learning on harder, more expensive design tasks.
    Here, we consider the general design setting of small-molecule lead optimization and create a spectrum of
post-training recipes that consist of varying mixtures of low-cost, low-fidelity design tasks and high-cost, high-
fidelity design tasks. On held-out evaluation design tasks—which are too expensive to train on—we find that
curriculum-based recipes that first train on low-fidelity tasks and later incorporate high-fidelity tasks perform best,
with recipes that only include high-fidelity tasks having poor performance. Additionally, we show that with these
curriculum-based recipes, we are able to post-train a small, 35-billion-parameter LLM that exceeds the performance
of much larger frontier models, including those that have themselves been extensively trained on small-molecule
tasks (Anthropic, 2026d). Taken together, the results here demonstrate that carefully constructed training recipes can
enable LLMs to effectively search through chemical space and generate promising molecular candidates that satisfy
complex, high-fidelity design objectives.


2 Related work
Transfer learning and post-training in chemistry and biology. Transfer learning (Pan and Yang, 2010; Yosinski et al.,
2014; Weiss et al., 2016) has been extensively used in chemical contexts, including training on large datasets of “low-
fidelity” properties and transferring to “high-fidelity” tasks (Buterez et al., 2024). Many early efforts used specialized
models, including Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) (Thölke and Fabritiis, 2022; Batzner et al., 2022; Batatia et al., 2023; Heid
et al., 2023). Recently, this has also been investigated in the context of post-training LLMs and LLM-like models for
chemical and biological applications, including on small-molecule design (Narayanan et al., 2025; Chennakesavalu,
Shmilovich, et al., 2026; Chennakesavalu, Hu, et al., 2025), protein design (Madani et al., 2023; Widatalla et al., 2024; Fallahpour
et al., 2026; Ibarraran et al., 2026), and biology (Istrate et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2026a; Anthropic, 2026d; Anthropic, 2026b).
These methods span both online RL methods on cheaply evaluable rewards and offline RL methods on expensive
rewards; however, none of these efforts have extensively investigated carrying out online RL to train models that can
effectively optimize computationally expensive properties.

Synthetic data scaling for post-training. Scaling training data has been demonstrated to be effective for improving
the performance of LLMs, both in the pretraining stage (Kaplan et al., 2020) and more recently in the post-training
stage; using synthetic data to scale post-training has become a popular strategy for improving on math and general
QA tasks (Guo et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025; Goldie et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026; NVIDIA et al., 2025), and has
been especially fruitful for scaling RL training for programming tasks (Le et al., 2022; Sancaktar et al., 2026) and terminal-
based tasks (Ivison et al., 2026; Merrill et al., 2026). Many data scaling strategies employ a curriculum learning (Elman,
1993; Krueger and Dayan, 2009; Bengio et al., 2009; Kumar et al., 2010; Hacohen and Weinshall, 2019) approach, where training
examples are staged according to difficulty with easier examples trained on first and harder examples trained on
last. This has been shown to improve generalization and performance compared to training directly on complex
examples because networks internalize foundational concepts that transfer positively for downstream tasks. More
recently, curriculum learning has been extended to reinforcement learning for large language models to enhance
reasoning and generalization (Qiu et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025; Bae et al., 2026; Parashar et al., 2026) where grouping
and scheduling tasks based on difficulty helps improve model performance and accuracy in verifiable settings,
specifically mathematics. Other approaches have found staged training of specialist models with a re-distillation
