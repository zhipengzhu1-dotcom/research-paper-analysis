4 Synthetic lead optimization tasks
In this work, we explore using LLMs for lead optimization tasks, where the goal is to optimize a starting seed
molecule towards desired properties. Additionally, we construct these tasks in such a way that the LLM is the
primary molecular designer but can still leverage external tools to help guide and inform the design process.
    Here, we consider the primary task of optimizing small molecules to have increased affinity for a predefined
protein receptor, while still respecting various physicochemical constraints. We approximate binding affinity using
SQM2.20 (Pecina et al., 2024), a universal physics-based scoring function using semiempirical quantum mechanical
methods, hereafter referred to as SQM. Compared with conventional scoring functions used in molecular docking
(e.g., Glide (Friesner et al., 2004), Vina (Trott and Olson, 2010), Chemgauss4 (McGann, 2011; McGann, 2012)), SQM
provides a more explicit treatment of the electronic effects governing protein–ligand interactions while remaining
computationally practical for large molecular systems. SQM has been shown to achieve accuracy comparable to
substantially more expensive DFT calculations and relatively strong agreement with experimental binding affinities
but at a higher computational cost than docking; a single SQM evaluation takes on the order of tens of minutes. At
the same time, higher-fidelity methods such as free energy perturbation (FEP), which are widely used for predicting
binding affinity changes during lead optimization (L. Wang et al., 2015; Ross et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2024), are substantially
more expensive and can require multiple GPU-hours per calculation. SQM therefore provides a useful middle
ground; it offers a substantial step up in fidelity over conventional docking-based scoring functions, while remaining
considerably cheaper than FEP. This tradeoff makes SQM more practical for our benchmark, although its cost is still
significant in an RLVR setting, where repeated tool calls and reward evaluations can result in stale rollouts during
training.
    In order to train the model to succeed in lead optimization tasks, we consider training the model on a hierar-
chy of synthetic molecular design tasks that follow the same abstraction—optimizing a property under a set of
constraints—and investigate whether training on these tasks can generalize to the harder and more expensive SQM
lead optimization tasks. Additionally, we explore various training recipes consisting of different training curricula,
task mixtures, and reward functions and examine how that impacts performance on SQM lead optimization tasks.
    We define two tiers of synthetic tasks of varying cost and simplicity. The first tier of tasks exclusively employs
RDKit (RDKit, 2026) and OpenEye (OpenEye, Cadence Molecular Sciences, 2025) oracles (see Appendix A for the full
set of oracles and tools), consisting of cheap cheminformatics oracles that range from simple atom counters to
more complex physicochemical descriptors (e.g., LogP, TPSA). We randomly construct tasks, where we sample the
property to optimize, whether to maximize or minimize the property, and a set of property constraints to satisfy,
where all properties, both the target and constraints, are computable by RDKit and/or OpenEye oracles. To ensure
that the sampled constraints are chemically plausible, we derive them from a molecule randomly sampled from
ZINC, a large database of commercially available compounds (Tingle et al., 2023). Finally, we filter out any generated
tasks with clear pathologies, such as trying to maximize or minimize a property that the starting molecule already
saturates or optimizing a non-scalar property. The core advantage of these tasks is that they can be rapidly generated
and are easily verifiable, providing a diverse and scalable dataset for model post-training. We refer to this set of
tasks as our “Tier-1” tasks.
